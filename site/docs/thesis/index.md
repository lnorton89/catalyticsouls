# The thesis: what it is and what it argues

**Title:** *Digital Media Technology, Synergy and Making It in Electronic Dance Music*
**Author:** Thomas W. Hughes (DJ TomFoolery), born September 27, 1978
**Degree context:** Southern Illinois University Carbondale, graduate project + paper (Radio-Television / mass communication; the document header reads "Digital Media"). Major professor Phylis Johnson; professors Todd Herreman and Jay Needham thanked.
**Written:** Fall 2007. The three industry interviews are dated October 18–24, 2007; every web reference was retrieved in October 2007.
**Length and shape:** about 22,000 words, no chapter numbers. A personal Foreword, a Statement of Purpose, a literature review with the author's memoir woven through it, a production narrative of the events, a Conclusion, 24 references, and a one-page Vita.

## The author in 2007

From the Foreword and Vita: raised in a small Alabama town; first psychedelic show Widespread Panic at the Fox in Atlanta, 1997; first event production a 1998 Auburn Hemp Alliance fundraiser; early Phish shows and the late-1990s New Orleans rave scene ("over 5,000 'kidds'"); a year in Orlando hoping to attend Full Sail, working Disney food service instead; stagehand for SFX and Clear Channel Entertainment while finishing an Economics BA at Auburn (2003, departmental award); self-taught on lighting consoles, Emu samplers, and vinyl mixing; record-buying trips to Satellite Records in Atlanta. Arrived at SIU in 2004 on a small inheritance as an undeclared graduate student to get at the Pro Tools labs; recorded local DJs' mix CDs; sat in on Mike Lescelius' sessions and a live multitrack at the Copper Dragon.

## The argument in four movements

### 1. Dance as ritual, the DJ as conduit

Using McNeill's *Keeping Together in Time*, Fikentscher's "disco concept," Thornton, and Hutson's anthropology of raves, Hughes frames underground dance music as modern communal rhythmic ritual with African American, gay, and Pentecostal roots. Recordings became a primary art form; the DJ makes mediated music immediate; "liveness is removed from the stage and placed on the dance floor." The DJ is "harmonic navigator" and, borrowing Jerry Garcia, "a conduit for life-energy." He defines "the vibe" with Fikentscher and then, in his own voice, as the cathedral: "colored light shooting through its stained glass, streaking through the incense smoke."

### 2. Rave versus club

Raves: one-off, all-night, multi-DJ, transformed or non-traditional spaces, unisex dress, classless crowd, infrequent attendance (so, he argues, lower addiction risk). Clubs: weekly or nightly, licensed, alcohol-driven, sexualized, one or two DJs. He traces house from Frankie Knuckles' Warehouse, acid house to the UK, techno from Detroit, Goa and psytrance, Florida breaks and UK nu-skool breaks, jungle and drum & bass. Crackdowns in the UK (1994) and US pushed rave into the club; a smaller next generation returned to fields and parking decks. In his account liquor-license holders lean on city councils to kill unlicensed events, "police are used to shut down completely legal and permitted events," and Underground Sound at Shawnee Cave is "the only all night rave of its magnitude still running in the Midwest," legal because of the venue's private-land character.

### 3. Government and media

The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee; the Criminal Justice Act's "succession of repetitive beats"; the 2007 London "raves are back" press cycle and DJmag's rebuttals; the 2001 New Orleans crack-house prosecution of Disco Donnie's promoters (thrown out); the 2002 RAVE Act (glow sticks and water as paraphernalia), its 2003 passage attached to the Amber Alert bill in conference, the Billings DEA intimidation weeks later; the 2005 Utah ACLU suit over the Versus raid. Then his own 2007: after the Insane Clown Posse's Gathering came to Southern Illinois that August, local media "looked for another easy story fix," ran "Techno Rave" headlines, and the sheriff complained on air about not being allowed to roam the property. Three heat-related ambulance calls at UGS 3 became "filled the local hospitals with drug overdoses."

### 4. Digital technology and the economics of the DJ/producer

The section that reads most like an economics thesis. As of October 2007:

- **Vinyl collapse.** Dance 12" sales down from 2,000–3,000 per release (c. 2005) to 300–600; techno "literally in the 100s" (McBride). Labels, distributors and stores closing; Satellite Records "recently defunct"; FACTORe (Jonathan Stewart) has a day job and hasn't released in a year. Shelf life: week-two sales used to be 80% of week one; now 20%.
- **The debate.** Woody McBride: digital is "catastrophic... I personally urged the main force behind digital technology, Richie Hawtin, to not push for this, likening it to Albert Einstein and his release of the atom bomb." Jorge Brea (Symphonic Distribution, founded 2006): digital "costs nothing but time," Beatport can break an unknown, witness "a Toronto producer known as deadmau5" who "came out of nowhere."
- **Beatport** at $1.99 a track, "officially not taking on any new labels"; CatalyticSouls got listed via Symphonic. His plan: a zero-upfront digital label that gathers demand data, then European vinyl (better mastering, exchange rate, prestige) as a booking tool rather than a profit center.
- **Rights.** Performance rights societies are "nearly worthless" for EDM; anonymity, pseudonyms, white labels and bootlegs are structural. Crossover hits (Chemical Brothers, Prodigy, Fatboy Slim, Dirty Vegas via Mitsubishi) keep the underground economy afloat.
- **The DJ/producer merger.** Digital mixing let producers who couldn't beatmatch play out; laptops made production cheap; "producer/DJs had taken over the circuit," non-producing DJs were "second class," and ghost production under DJ names was normal. His response: learn Reason and Ableton Live, commission a Transformerman remix of his track "Defiance" so a known UK name is attached, and remix stems from top UK producers.

## The production narrative (2004–2007), in brief

- **Spring 2004.** Cold-calls Bob Goodale at the cave; booked to play "disco" after a country/southern rock show; plays the Doors, Head East and a sunrise Grateful Dead set on vinyl borrowed from WDBX; fires the headliner's sound crew; wins Goodale over.
- **Summer 2004.** Digs ditches at the cave; volunteers at Woody McBride's Caveman Experience (Goodale says $50,000, seven stages); stops drinking that weekend and never resumes; cleans the 52 acres afterward.
- **Fall 2004.** Founds Fool'n'Around Sound; CaveStock 1. Pink Floyd tribute headliner bails three weeks out; flyers a 40-mile radius alone; runs sound alone; break-even; learns The Nightlife magazine drove 90% of coupon redemptions.
- **Winter–spring 2005.** Creates the CatalyticSouls brand as an umbrella "for any persons or groups that wanted to be a part of what was happening." Cave Fest draws ~1,400 (the Southern's preview projected ~800).
- **Summer 2005.** A months-long online campaign against him by the Chicago promoter of Caveman Experience; a rival "true Midwest" camp fest; a friend leaking his plans. A regional performer buy-in plan fails. Disco Donnie signs on as talent buyer. UGS 1 (June 17–19): 150+ artists on eight stages, 18+, $40, ~1,200 attendance against 1,500–2,000 projected, a large loss floated by Disco.
- **Fall 2005–winter 2006.** CaveStock 2 turns a profit; four-city club run (St. Louis, Memphis, Jackson, Birmingham); takes every gig regardless of pay; breaks "almost taboo" in the Midwest; pays Disco off with loans.
- **Spring 2006.** Cave Fest breaks 2,000. 1,000 pro-pressed *USAmnesia* mix CDs given away, mastered for cars, aimed at 18-to-21-year-olds.
- **Summer 2006.** UGS 2: Infected Mushroom, Electric Skychurch, SwitchFocus. Four stages instead of eight, each run by a regional crew. Sound from Milwaukee, a rebuilt full-color laser from Memphis projecting artist names and the CatalyticSouls logo onto scrim and cliffs, a 40-foot video screen, one noise complaint resolved by retuning. The crew's DJs (Andy B, TomFoolery, Unkl Ryan) take the post-sunset Friday slots nobody wanted and play to 1,000+. "Mildly successful financially, but hugely successful in other areas."
- **2007.** UGS 3 and the "Techno Rave" press (The Southern Illinoisan reported at least four arrests over the weekend; the thesis says none were made at the venue). Gamma Fest in Arkansas with Shpongle and Hallucinogen, unmentioned in the thesis. The label launches (September 30, 2007). Reason and Ableton. The Transformerman remix.

## The conclusion

> After years of working in the electronic music business, I have yet to net a profit on electronic events. This paper has led me to try to define what it is about the music and the scene that I invest so many resources into promoting. I found that perhaps it is simply the right to be undefined.
