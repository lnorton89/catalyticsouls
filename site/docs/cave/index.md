# Shawnee Salt Petre Cave / Shawnee Cave Amphitheater, 1969–2026

**Where:** 3747 Illinois Route 127 ("Black Diamond Highway"), about seven miles south of Murphysboro, near Pomona and across from Cedar Lake, Jackson County, Illinois. Roughly 50–70 acres bordered on three sides by the Shawnee National Forest, under a mile from Natural Bridge.
**What:** a sandstone bluff shelter, 200-plus feet across and about 125 feet high, that faces a sloping lawn: "Illinois' largest natural rock shelter." Not an enclosed cave. Saltpeter (potassium nitrate) was reputedly mined from its floor for gunpowder; Native American use as shelter is traditional. A waterfall runs after rain; catfish live in the adjacent stream.
**Names over time:** Salt Petre Cave Park, Saltpetre / Saltpeter Cave, Shawnee Salt Petre Cave Natural Amphitheatre, Shawnee Saltpetre Cave Theater, Shawnee Natural Amphitheatre, "the Cave," and since 2020 Shawnee Cave Amphitheater.

The thesis's version of the place: "215-foot bluff shelter," "50 acres with a view. There're not fences everywhere," and the reason Underground Sound could run all night legally: private land, no liquor license to threaten, a landlord who wanted the shows.

## 1. Gib Todd's venue (1969–late 1980s)

Gib Todd of Murphysboro built the stage and made the cave a regional stop. The Daily Egyptian in 2000 dated organized concerts from 1974 at "four or five a year"; other sources say 1969. Documented or remembered acts: Ozark Mountain Daredevils, Steve Miller Band, Head East, Dr. Hook & the Medicine Show, T.G. Sheppard, Tanya Tucker at 15 (a 1976 clipping survives), and Muddy Waters (a setlist is logged for September 17, 1978). Bob Goodale, then an SIU student, helped organize the 1978 "Shawnee Jamboree" there. Concerts stopped in the late 1980s "when the owner was no longer able to carry the tradition on." Through the 1990s it was open for paddle-boating; Shane Wade remembers visiting by paddle boat in junior high.

## 2. Caveman Bob (2000–2011)

Robert "Caveman Bob" Goodale and his wife Lou took over around 2000 ("We're bringing the cave back to the 70s"), reportedly buying cheaply after Goodale saved the previous owner's life. Goodale's roots were in Centralia; his businesses had been ice cream, coal, and firewood. His website called the venue a non-profit with a capacity of 4,000. Nazareth played July 4, 2002. In summer 2003 came the first Caveman Experience, an electronic camp-out; in July 2004 the second, with Christopher Lawrence, Grooverider, Dieselboy and Woody McBride, camping capped at 400 tents. Hughes volunteered at that one and started his own series that fall.

**The Catalytic Souls years (2004–2008)** are on their own pages: [Cave Fest & CaveStock](/catalytic-souls/cave-fest-cavestock) and [Underground Sound](/catalytic-souls/underground-sound). The thesis describes Goodale as a "clever businessman" who kept a notebook of every band's rate so that "when one promoter left him he could continue with minimal set back," which is exactly what he did after 2008. Catalytic Souls' website served as the cave's calendar in those years ("your web connection to Caveman Bob and Gettin Lucky Productions"), and Gettin' Lucky Productions ran its own Blues Fest and Cave Rock Festival there in 2005. In July 2007 The Southern Illinoisan reported at least four arrests during Underground Sound 3 and a private security firm, hired jointly by Goodale and the promoters, saying it had burned confiscated drugs and knives; that is the coverage the thesis calls the "Techno Rave" cycle.

Other Goodale-era programming (from his own site, cavefest.fws1.com, and the local press): HonkyTonk Homeslice with Bill Nershi of String Cheese Incident (Aug 2006); Cave Blast metal/country nights (2006, 2011); New Grass Bloozegas and the New Riders of the Purple Sage (2006); Monsters of Tribute (June 2007), organized by Goodale, who the Daily Egyptian said "has coordinated events at the venue since the 1970s"; Stripminesfest (2007) and the Strip Mines Music Festival (Aug 2008, ~550 paid, Family Groove Company, Future Rock, Backyard Tire Fire, Garaj Mahal); Halloween at the Cave (2008). A JamBase writer in 2008 described Goodale as looking like Santa Claus "in shorts and an unbuttoned Hawaiian shirt."

## The summer of 2007 in the local paper

The Southern Illinoisan's two July 2007 stories are the only contemporaneous outside account of what the neighbors and the county thought. The first (July 9, Adam Testa, "Behavior in local cave drawing ire: At least four arrested during weekend festival") opens with a neighbor on Tomcat Hill Road who let his field grow knee-high as a barrier against campers wandering onto his land. Sgt. Dave Nichols of the Jackson County Sheriff's Department: "Every time they have that particular type of festival, several times ambulances will get called down there." Three ambulance runs, five patients at St. Joseph Memorial in Murphysboro, at least four arrests for cannabis, controlled substances or paraphernalia. Goodale, who told the paper the venue hosted six events a year, put it on growth rather than the music: Underground Sound "has grown from attracting about 600 people to more than 2,000... A few bad apples spoil it," and the ambulances were "more to do with heat exhaustion." Deputies could not patrol private property, so they watched Route 127. A 17-year-old from Chicago was chased into the woods by a group from the concert and located by phone. The second story (July 10, "Security claims it burned confiscated drugs, weapons") quoted Dennis Karnes of Five-"O" Event Security Services, the firm Goodale and the promoters had hired: keeping contraband out "would have been impossible," and the firm said it had burned what it took off about fifteen people. Read against the thesis's own account of the same weekend (three heat calls, arrests "none... made at the venue," a sheriff complaining about access), the two versions agree on the facts and differ on the frame, which is the thesis's whole argument about media in miniature. Full text of the first article is in the repository under `research/raw/press/`.

## Cavetronic: the year after (July 2009)

Catalytic Souls' departure did not end electronic music at the cave. On July 17–18, 2009, a Clarksville, Tennessee crew called Electronic Assault, with partners billed as "SSI," ran **Cavetronic** at the cave ("representin Mid Tenn and N. Utah"), with DJ Shortee and DJ Ronin among the performers; a promo clip titled "Cavetronic 2" suggests a first edition in 2008, and a Nashville DJ's bio describes "Cavetronic (Rave in the Cave)" as running from summer 2008 to winter 2009. Video from the July 2009 weekend survives (see [Media](/media)). This is almost certainly part of what Shane Wade meant by "2008 or 2009": the cave's rave era outlived the crew that built it by at least one summer, under promoters from out of state.

## 3. Decline (2008–2012)

The markers, in order:

- October 13, 2007: "Freakenstein – Shawnee Electro-Jam," a "$12 night at the Cave" that Catalytic Souls hosted "to show our appreciation for all of the love and support given to us over the years" (Andy B, TomFoolery, Reperkushin, Dave Skeezy, Tommy Gunns, "the CatalyticSouls & iLevel Light Show"). In hindsight it reads like a farewell.
- From the March 21, 2008 capture onward, Goodale's website carried a standing banner: "Help Save The Cave — Volunteers Needed," later "Forestry, Electrician, Help Needed." The site was kept by a volunteer, Sherry Cosby, who posted "Find Yourself at the Cave" photo pages and asked attendees to "show the cave some love" by cleaning up. The clean-up problem was old: after Cave Fest 2005 a forum poster found "Bob (the owner) and his wife... left to clean up a shitload of garbage" and tried to start a volunteer crew.
- October 31–November 1, 2008: "Halloween at the Cave," co-presented by Catalytic Souls and Gettin' Lucky, the crew's last co-billing at the venue.
- Spring 2009: Catalytic Souls withdrew from Cave Fest "due to unspecified issues." Underground Sound had already moved to Missouri.
- August 2010: a Sierra Club hiker found $5 admission, boating discontinued "due to insurance concerns," and an owner whose main business was firewood plus festivals.
- Goodale kept the spring 420 Show and fall Harvest Fest going with regional jam bands and a dubstep or techno side stage through April 2011, plus a July 2011 Caveblast, the last event on his site.
- The 2011 420 Show page shows the last shape of the cave's electronic side: "Main Stage will be Jam Bands and Second Stage will be Dubstep," with a dozen St. Louis and Springfield dubstep DJs booked through Digital-Glitch.
- August 31, 2012: the same hiker's postscript: gate closed, "for sale" sign. Goodale's website was never updated after Caveblast (July 2011) and by 2023 its host displayed "Site Disabled... due to billing issue."
- September 14, 2012: setlist.fm logs one more show (House of Heroes), context unknown.

Shane Wade, 2023: "In the 2000s, it became an underground EDM scene, which to be honest with you kind of got out of hand with some of the partying that the kids down there from the college did. By probably 2008 or 2009 it shut down. My partner acquired it in 2012 or 2013 and then we just let it sit for a while, let the smoke clear from some of the stuff that happened there at the end. The city leaders had a bad taste in their mouths from that." Showcaves.com's listing, written in the 2000s, already noted that "violence and drug use" at concerts had prompted "regular discussions among police and local politicians." The 2020 Southern Illinoisan called Goodale "the last face of the cave." (One trade article gives the purchase year as 2004; every other source, including the buyer's partner, says 2012 or 2013.) No obituary or current information for Bob Goodale was found.

Wade's "2008 or 2009" and the events record agree: the cave did not literally close then, but the era that defined it did. The last Underground Sound at the cave was August 2008; the last Catalytic Souls Cave Fest was spring 2008; Goodale ran three more diminishing seasons alone.

## 4. Revival (2020–2024)

Jeff Parrish (owner) and Shane Wade (manager and co-owner; grew up in Herrin, lives in Marion, attended Carterville High) let it sit, then used the 2020 lockdown to rebuild: stage tripled to 50 by 50 feet, new drainage, concrete for tour trailers, 20,000 square feet of turf, a liquor license, an RV park and glamping. Renamed the Shawnee Cave Amphitheater, it reopened October 31, 2020 with a socially distanced Yonder Mountain String Band show. Wade: "I didn't know the first thing about it until the summer of 2020... somebody said 'You've gotta get Billy Strings here,' so I listened to Billy Strings." He booked jamgrass on the evidence: "these bluegrass jam bands have so much engagement from the fans... I swear there was not one piece of trash" in the leased parking field after his first festival.

Wade's stated ceiling: "my events are less than three thousand people. There might be a day where I go to five thousand but to be honest with you, I like it at three." His description of the audience: "kind of a hippy crowd, a lot of jam bands. There's a little bit of electronic but not too much."

That last sentence is the venue's verdict on its own 2000s.

## 5. Dark again (August 2024–present)

On August 6, 2024, the amphitheater announced on Facebook that Weekend at the Cave was cancelled, that ticket and vendor refunds were being issued, thanked performers, staff, vendors and supporters, said it would "cherish the memories made at the venue," gave no reason, and said it was closed for the season with no 2025 dates. As of September 2026, Songkick, JamBase, Vivid Seats and TicketSmarter list zero upcoming events, and none has anything after that cancelled weekend. The venue's website fails its TLS handshake. A local Facebook group thread asks "What happened to the Shawnee Cave Amphitheater?" No sale has been reported.

## 6. The cave and the thesis

The thesis's central practical claim was that this venue's private, unlicensed, out-of-town character made it the one place an all-night electronic camp-out could survive in the Midwest. The record since says two things about that. First, it was true, for about five summers, and it depended entirely on one landlord's tolerance and local politics not turning. Second, when the same site reopened, it reopened with a liquor license, a 3,000 cap, an alcohol-free branded festival, and "not too much" electronic music. The thesis argued that "liquor license-holding club owners pressuring city councils" pushed dance music into clubs. The cave's own arc suggests the pressure works on landlords too.
