# Scorecard: the thesis's claims against the record

Each row pairs a claim or prediction from October 2007 with what happened through September 2026. Sources for the right-hand column are in the six topic pages and the [industry agent report](/research/agent-report-industry).

| Thesis claim (2007) | What happened (2008–2026) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Vinyl for dance music is dying; 300–600 sales per 12" is a collapse. | 2006 was the all-time US vinyl bottom (858,000 LPs). Growth every year since: $1.04 billion and 46.8 million units in 2025, 19 straight years, more than three times CD units. A 300-unit pressing is now the standard, profitable indie dance run because of pre-orders and Bandcamp. What died was the **download**: US download revenue fell to $221.8M in 2025, roughly a fifth of vinyl. Juno Download shut in June 2026. | Right mechanism, wrong direction |
| Digital stores (Beatport) are the future of dance distribution. | True for about a decade, then overtaken by streaming. Beatport survived by becoming a subscription piped into Rekordbox and Serato, plus a software company (Loopmasters, Plugin Boutique, IMS). IMS puts the global electronic music business at $15.1B in 2025. | Half right |
| Start a zero-cost digital label to gather data, then press European vinyl as a booking tool. | The label ran 14 releases in three years and never pressed vinyl. The playbook itself became universal (DistroKid, Bandcamp, Symphonic), and "European vinyl for prestige at 300 copies" is exactly what small dance labels do now. | Right plan, not executed |
| McBride: digital "has killed a huge part of the industry." Brea: it "allows new producers to effectively get out there." | Both. Thousands of vinyl-era labels died. Symphonic alone now services half a million artists. Hughes' own synthesis, that the bedroom-producer boom would push production standards up "exponentially," is now the industry's central complaint. | Right |
| Shelf life has collapsed to days because of file sharing. | Collapsed further, for a different reason: algorithmic streaming and TikTok. In 2023 the four most-popular US TikTok songs were all sped-up remixes. | Right, wrong cause |
| Producer/DJs have taken over; non-producing DJs are second class; ghost production is normal. | Fully realized. Ghost production has published price lists ($300–2,500 a track). Stems separation (2022–24) made every DJ a live remixer. AI generators (Suno, Udio) settled with two majors in late 2025 and will do the ghost's job for pennies. | Right |
| Laptops and CDJs replace vinyl in the booth; Richie Hawtin is the vanguard. | The laptop lost too. The booth standardized on Pioneer (now AlphaTheta) CDJs and USB sticks; Traktor, Hawtin's platform, is a niche product. New Zealand blocked AlphaTheta's purchase of Serato in 2024 to stop one company owning the dominant hardware and software. Vinyl-only DJing survives as a deliberate niche, the "prestige" role the thesis predicted. | Half right |
| The rave has been forced into the club; the Midwest's warehouse scenes are dead; Shawnee Cave is the last legal all-nighter. | The 2010–16 boom was the rave going corporate: EDC from a Los Angeles rave to 525,000 people in Las Vegas (2025) with Live Nation owning half of Insomniac; SFX rolled up Disco Donnie, ID&T, Electric Zoo and Beatport, IPO'd in 2013, bankrupt by 2016. Then Gen Z reinvented the warehouse rave (Boiler Room, hard techno at 150+ BPM, Berlin techno on UNESCO's list in 2024) while nightclubs closed at record rates. The Midwest camping rave became Electric Forest (2011–), at 45,000 people. | Wrong |
| Media sensationalism and the RAVE Act make promotion legally and financially precarious. | No prosecution has ever been brought under the RAVE Act, but its chill was real enough that DOJ issued 2018 guidance saying free water and drug education don't violate it, after Dede Goldsmith's campaign following her daughter's 2013 death. 45 states legalized fentanyl test strips. Biden never amended his own law. The crack-house statute the thesis said was "thrown out" in New Orleans seized Camp Zoe in 2010, the site of UGS 5 and 6. | Right, and then some |
| Liquor-license holders and local politics kill unlicensed events. | Shane Wade on why the cave sat empty a decade: "The city leaders had a bad taste in their mouths." The revived venue has a liquor license and programs bluegrass. | Right |
| Crossover hits keep the underground economy afloat. | Inverted after 2012: the crossover was the economy (Skrillex's three Grammys, Calvin Harris's $66M years) and the underground defined itself against it. Hard techno and drum & bass had real US crossover moments in 2024. | Backwards |
| Performance rights societies are nearly worthless for EDM. | Still largely true for DJ-tool tracks; streaming royalties and sync are now real revenue, and Beatport's data feeds label accounting in ways that didn't exist. | Mostly still true |
| Winter Music Conference is "a gathering of over 20,000." | Split from Ultra in 2011, reacquired 2018, held 2019, five-year hiatus, back in 2025–26 as a small conference. Amsterdam Dance Event drew 600,000 in 2025. | Overtaken |
| Camping festivals need "multiple peaks and valleys with genre changes every hour or two." | Now the default multi-stage format from Electric Forest to Shambhala. | Right |
| The "vibe": the DJ as conduit, the crowd as the performance, the event as something that "exists only through the thoughts and actions of the participants." | Aged best. It is what post-COVID festivals mean by "intimacy," what Wade means by "not a bad seat in the house," and why "the right to be undefined" reads better in 2026 than most of the numbers around it. | Right, and not a prediction |

## Three ironies the scorecard can't hold

1. **The stagehand and the roll-up.** Hughes worked as a stagehand for SFX and Clear Channel Entertainment in the early 2000s. Clear Channel Entertainment became Live Nation in 2005; Robert Sillerman relaunched SFX in 2012 to buy Disco Donnie Presents, the very company that backstopped Underground Sound. In April 2026 a jury found Live Nation liable on every antitrust count in the states' case.
2. **The Full Sail loop.** In 1999 he couldn't raise the money to attend. By 2020 he was Course Director of Music Production there.
3. **The atom bomb.** McBride told Hughes he'd begged Hawtin not to push digital, "likening it to Albert Einstein and his release of the atom bomb." Hawtin's platform lost the format war anyway, to a USB stick.
