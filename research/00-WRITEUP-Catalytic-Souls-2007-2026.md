# Catalytic Souls, Underground Sound, and the Cave
## Tom Hughes' 2007 SIU thesis, read against everything that happened next (2007–2026)

*Compiled September 16, 2026. Sources for every claim are in the companion files in this folder: `01-primary-findings-log.md` (what I found directly), and `agent-reports/` (three research reports on the crew, the venue, and the industry). Where something is inferred rather than documented, it is marked as such.*

---

## 0. Read this first

Three things you should know before the rest of this document, because they frame everything:

1. **Tom Hughes appears to have died in early 2022.** On February 5, 2022, his Bay Area Records collaborator Lo IQ? posted: "A sweet and kind soul has passed Tom Hughes aka Tom Foolery i will miss you." I could not find a newspaper obituary. The date is consistent with the paper trail: his Florida LLC filed its last annual report in January 2021 and was administratively dissolved in September 2022, and his Facebook page's last post is from February 2021. If you didn't know this, I'm sorry to be the one delivering it in a research document.

2. **The thesis is not a bystander's account.** It is the founder's own record of Catalytic Souls' first three years (2004–2007), written in the middle of it, as the paper half of an SIU Carbondale master's project. That makes it a primary source, with a primary source's blind spots: it anonymizes rivals, it rounds numbers, and it was written before the ending.

3. **Almost none of the world it describes still exists in the form it describes.** The vinyl economy it mourned came back. The download stores it bet on are dying. The cave it built its brand on closed, sat empty for a decade, reopened as a bluegrass venue, and is dark again. The all-night camping rave model it defended migrated to a Missouri campground that the DEA seized under the very statute the thesis spent pages attacking. And the man who wrote that he "had yet to net a profit on electronic events" ended up teaching music production at the school he couldn't afford to attend in 1999.

## 1. The thesis itself

**Title:** *Digital Media Technology, Synergy and Making It in Electronic Dance Music*
**Author:** Thomas W. Hughes (DJ TomFoolery), born September 27, 1978. BA Economics, Auburn University, May 2003 (Excellence in Economics Award). Address at time of writing: Makanda, Illinois.
**Program:** Southern Illinois University Carbondale, graduate project + paper (Radio-Television / mass communication track; the document header reads "Digital Media"). Major professor Phylis Johnson; professors Todd Herreman and Jay Needham thanked.
**Date:** Fall 2007. The three industry interviews are dated October 18–24, 2007, and all web references were retrieved in October 2007.
**Format:** ~22,000 words in a single document with no chapter numbering: a personal Foreword, a Statement of Purpose, a literature review woven through with memoir, then a production narrative of the events, and a Conclusion. The reference list is 24 items, leaning on Fikentscher, Thornton, Brewster & Broughton, Hesmondhalgh, Hutson, McNeill, Attali, Passman, and Beverly May's *Canadian Theatre Review* piece on Richie Hawtin.

### What the thesis argues

The argument runs in four movements.

**Dance as ritual, DJ as conduit.** Drawing on McNeill's *Keeping Together in Time*, Fikentscher's "disco concept," and Hutson's anthropology of raves, Hughes frames underground dance music as a modern instance of communal rhythmic ritual with African American, gay, and Pentecostal roots. The DJ is "harmonic navigator" and "utility... conduit for life-energy" (he borrows Jerry Garcia's line). "Liveness is removed from the stage and placed on the dance floor."

**Rave versus club.** Raves are one-offs in transformed or non-traditional spaces, all-night, multi-DJ, unisex, classless, low-frequency for participants (so lower addiction risk, he argues). Clubs are weekly, licensed, alcohol-driven, sexualized, one-or-two-DJ affairs. UK Criminal Justice Act crackdowns (1994) and US crackdowns pushed rave back into the club; in his telling, liquor-license holders lean on city councils to kill unlicensed events, and "police are used to shut down completely legal and permitted events." He positions Underground Sound at Shawnee Cave as "the only all night rave of its magnitude still running in the Midwest," legal because of the venue's unique private-land status.

**Government and media.** The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee, the CJA's "succession of repetitive beats," the 2001 New Orleans crack-house prosecution of Disco Donnie, the 2002 RAVE Act (glow sticks and water as paraphernalia), its 2003 passage attached to the Amber Alert bill, the 2003 Billings DEA intimidation, the 2005 Utah ACLU suit. He then describes his own experience: after the Insane Clown Posse "Gathering" came to Southern Illinois in summer 2007, local media "looked for another easy story fix," ran "Techno Rave" headlines, and the sheriff complained on air about not being allowed to roam the property. He reports three heat-related ambulance calls at UGS 3 that became "filled the local hospitals with drug overdoses" in the coverage.

**Digital technology and the economics of the DJ/producer.** This is the part that reads most like an economics thesis. His claims, as of October 2007:
- Dance 12" sales collapsed from 2,000–3,000 per release (c. 2005) to 300–600, and in techno "literally in the 100s" (McBride). Labels, distributors and stores were closing; Satellite Records in Atlanta was "recently defunct." Producers like FACTORe had taken day jobs. Shelf life of a track had shrunk from weeks to days because of file sharing.
- Woody McBride called digital DJing "catastrophic" and said he'd personally begged Richie Hawtin not to push it, "likening it to Albert Einstein and his release of the atom bomb."
- Jorge Brea (Symphonic Distribution, founded 2006) took the opposite view: digital lowers costs to "nothing but time," and Beatport can break an unknown, citing "a Toronto producer known as deadmau5" who "came out of nowhere."
- Beatport at $1.99/track was the dominant store, "officially not taking on any new labels"; CatalyticSouls got listed through Symphonic. His plan: run a zero-upfront-cost digital label to gather demand data, then press vinyl in Europe (better mastering, stronger sales, better exchange rate) as a booking tool rather than a profit center.
- Performance rights societies are "nearly worthless" for EDM; anonymity, pseudonyms, white labels and bootlegs are structural, not incidental. Crossover hits (Chemical Brothers, Prodigy, Fatboy Slim, Dirty Vegas via Mitsubishi) keep the underground economy afloat.
- Technology had merged DJ and producer: "producer/DJs had taken over the circuit," non-producing DJs were "second class," and ghost-produced tracks under DJ names were common. His response was to learn Reason and Ableton Live, commission a Transformerman remix of his track "Defiance" to get a known UK name attached, and remix stems from top UK producers.

**The production narrative.** Spring 2004: cold-calls Bob Goodale at Shawnee Salt Petre Cave, gets an after-hours "disco" slot at a country/southern rock show, plays the Doors, Head East and a sunrise Grateful Dead set off borrowed WDBX vinyl, fires the headliner's sound crew, and wins Goodale over. Summer 2004: digs ditches at the cave, volunteers at Woody McBride's Caveman Experience (which he says ran over $50,000 with seven stages), stops drinking that weekend. Fall 2004: founds Fool'n'Around Sound, throws CaveStock 1; the Pink Floyd tribute headliner bails three weeks out; he flyers a 40-mile radius alone and runs sound alone; breaks even, learns that The Nightlife magazine drove 90% of coupon redemptions. Spring 2005: creates the CatalyticSouls brand as an umbrella so "any persons or groups" could join; Cave Fest draws ~1,400. Summer 2005: fights a months-long online smear campaign from the Chicago promoter behind Caveman Experience; a friend leaks his plans to a rival "true Midwest" camp fest; Disco Donnie signs on as talent buyer; UGS 1 books 150+ artists on eight stages, draws ~1,200 against a 1,500–2,000 projection at $40, and loses far more than the $5,000–7,000 he'd budgeted to lose; Disco floats the shortfall. Fall 2005 through winter: CaveStock 2 turns a profit, funds a four-city club run (St. Louis, Memphis, Jackson, Birmingham) packaging national acts with TomFoolery as support; he takes every gig regardless of pay, becomes a top name in Mississippi, and finds breaks "almost taboo" in the Midwest. Spring 2006: Cave Fest breaks 2,000; releases 1,000 pro-pressed *USAmnesia* mix CDs as free promotion aimed at 18-to-21-year-olds. Summer 2006: UGS 2 with Infected Mushroom, Electric Skychurch and SwitchFocus; four stages instead of eight, each run by a regional crew; sound from Milwaukee, a rebuilt full-color laser from Memphis projecting artist names and the CatalyticSouls logo onto scrim and cliff faces, a 40-foot video screen; one noise complaint resolved by retuning a stage; the crew's own DJs (Andy B, TomFoolery, Unkl Ryan) take the just-after-sunset Friday slots nobody wanted and play to 1,000+. "Mildly successful financially, but hugely successful in other areas."

**The conclusion.** "After years of working in the electronic music business, I have yet to net a profit on electronic events... I found that perhaps it is simply the right to be undefined."

### What the external record adds to the thesis's own account

Independent sources confirm the skeleton and add names the thesis withheld or omitted:

- **The Southern Illinoisan, April 7, 2005** previewed the "Gemini Moon Cave Fest" (April 15–16, 2005) with Tom Foolery quoted as organizer, Bob and Lou Goodale quoted as owners, ~800 expected, $12/$20, and www.catalyticsouls.com printed. The electronic stage ("Beyond the Moon") lineup is preserved: Andy B (house, Chicago), Tom Foolery (breaks, Carbondale), Spacehead (breaks, Carbondale), plus DJs from St. Louis, Cape Girardeau, Kentucky, Arizona, Effingham and Bloomington. The band stage had Jake's Leg (Dead tribute), Helping Phriendly (Phish tribute), DreamLab (Floyd tribute), Mothership Orchestra, G-String Hypothesis, and others. Note the ~800 figure in the preview versus the ~1,400 the thesis reports; both can be true (projection vs. turnout).
- **Caveman Experience 2, July 16–18, 2004** was publicly organized by Intergrüv Network, Middleschool Productions and The Genius of Fun (Woody McBride's brand), with Christopher Lawrence, Grooverider, Dieselboy and DJ ESP live, at $25/$35 with camping capped at 400 tents. The thesis's "seven stages" and "$50,000" are Goodale's numbers relayed by Hughes; listings say 10 stages or "60+ DJs."
- **The Daily Egyptian, September 30, 2004** covered a band from Peoria playing to a thinned-out crowd at 2 a.m. after their set was pushed back at a cave camp-out. That's almost certainly CaveStock 1, the night Hughes describes running sound alone.
- **UGS 1** was June 17–19, 2005 ("Midwest Freakfest," nine stages per one attendee, George Acosta's *The Lost World* CD release on the bill). **UGS 2** was June 30–July 2, 2006 with Infected Mushroom and Electric Skychurch, confirmed by a contemporaneous Shroomery thread ("it is in a fucking cave"). **UGS 3** was July 6–8, 2007: a photo report lists 100+ DJs including Uberzone, Aphrodite, Jackal & Hyde, Skylab2000, DJ Funk and Reid Speed, VJs Benji Ramsey and 8-Bit, and a fire troupe.
- **Cave Fest '07** (April 20–22, 2007) had Vince Herman of Leftover Salmon, DJ ESP Woody McBride, Cornmeal and Defined Perception; Hughes told the Daily Egyptian, "I want people to celebrate nature and the Earth in a pristine environment." **CaveStock 3** (Sept 22–23, 2006) had the Chicago Afrobeat Project; **CaveStock 4** (Oct 5–6, 2007) was co-billed with Goodale ("Santa").
- **The Insane Clown Posse event** the thesis mentions was the first Gathering of the Juggalos at Hogrock Ranch in Cave-In-Rock, Illinois, August 9–12, 2007, about 90 miles east of the cave. Four years later Hughes would hold Underground Sound 7 on that same ranch.
- **The 2007 "Techno Rave" coverage** was located late in the session (The Southern Illinoisan, July 9–10, 2007: at least four arrests; a security firm burning confiscated drugs and knives); see section 2. Only headlines and lead paragraphs were readable; the full text is paywalled.
- A 2005 crew page from Spoololife, a regional rave collective, describes Tom Foolery as "one of the most prominent breaks DJs in the central United States... influenced by the hard/funky south and the deep/progressive mid-west," notes he opened for Kaskade and played peak hours at Tampa's Amphitheater, and quotes his model of the DJ "as a spiritual guide" in the mold of Paul van Dyk, Carl Cox and Uberzone. That is the same theology as the thesis, in flyer form.

Two things stand out. First, the label launched the month before the thesis interviews were conducted, so "I have recently set up one of those myself" was literally true. Second, the thesis's stated plan for the label as a multi-artist, multi-genre imprint that would gather sales data and graduate to European vinyl never happened. It stayed a one-artist digital outlet, and no CatalyticSouls vinyl exists on Discogs. The catalog-number gaps (CS001, 007, 009, 012...) suggest either planned releases that never shipped or numbering shared with a distributor.

### Underground Sound 4: the last one at the cave

August 15–17, 2008, "Circus of Freaks," Shawnee Cave, $45 presale / $60 door, "Hosted by CatalyticSouls." Headliners were Hallucinogen live, Shpongle DJ set, and Skazi, which is to say the psytrance escalation from Infected Mushroom in 2006 continued: Simon Posford's two projects were about as big a get as that scene had. Spring 2008 also had Cave Fest 4 ("The Original 420 Show," hosted by CatalyticSouls, with a techno tent and TomFoolery on the bill) and a "Summer Cavefest" in June with TomFoolery, Andy B and The Dervish.

By April 2009 the relationship had ended. A Rollitup forum thread about Cave Fest 2009 notes that Catalytic Souls had been involved but "withdrew due to unspecified issues" before the event. Goodale carried the 420 Show and Harvest Fest on without them through 2011 (a 2011 flyer lists a "dubstep second stage run by Digital-Glitch," so the electronic component survived the divorce). No source states the reason for the split; Shane Wade's later account (section 4) that the EDM era "got out of hand" and left "city leaders" with "a bad taste in their mouths" is the closest thing to an explanation on the record.

### Camp Zoe (2009–2010)

Underground Sound 5 (August 14–15, 2009) moved 200 miles west to Camp Zoe in Salem, Missouri, the 330-acre campground owned by Jimmy Tebeau of The Schwag and home of Schwagstock. The promotional copy called it "the nation's premier electronic dance music camp fest," now in "a new location full of its own natural wonders," with "all of the amenities of a full scale festival." The lineup was the biggest the series ever had: Bassnectar, Astral Projection, Donald Glaude, EOTO, Fort Knox Five, Tech Itch, Woody McBride, Keith MacKenzie, TomFoolery, Andy B, The Dervish. Tickets were $70/$100 and Camp Zoe's capacity was 10,000. Theme camps appeared for the first time ("Magic Carpet Ride," an Arabian lounge with 15,000 watts of its own sound, run by 1family productions and Janky Inc.), which tells you the Burning Man vocabulary had arrived.

Underground Sound 6 (August 13–15, 2010, "Crossing the Event Horizon") leaned harder into the bass-music wave then cresting: Excision, Bro Safari, Evol Intent live, VibeSquaD, ill.gates, Heyoka, Zilla, alongside Rabbit in the Moon 3D, EOTO, Charles Feelgood, Angel Alanis, Woody McBride, TomFoolery and Andy B. Jamie Janover hosted as "emissary" for Nassim Haramein's Resonance Project, with a unified-field-theory presentation between sets. Weekend passes at the gate ran $80–$140.

Eleven weeks later, on November 1, 2010, the DEA and Missouri Highway Patrol raided Camp Zoe after a four-year investigation. Tebeau was charged with "maintaining a drug-involved premises," the crack-house statute, the exact law the RAVE Act had extended to promoters and that the thesis spends two pages attacking. Because prosecutors could never show Tebeau sold anything himself, the charge was that he knowingly allowed sales on his land. He pleaded, served 30 months, paid $50,000, and the federal government took the property by civil forfeiture. It is now Echo Bluff State Park. Camp Zoe's own website still reads "NO EVENTS SCHEDULED FOR 2011."

The thesis had written, about Utah in 2005: "No promoter or venue can successfully put on concerts if they never know when or why the cops will end an event." Five years later the venue the series had moved to was ended permanently.

### Hogrock (2011) and the end

Underground Sound 7, "The Freaky Tiki," July 21–24, 2011, at Hogrock Ranch, Cave-In-Rock, Illinois: the Juggalos' site, 8,000–10,000 lawn capacity, "swimming, primitive camping, RV hook ups, running water, and shower houses." Headliners across sources: Nigel Richards, Noel Sanger, Keith MacKenzie, Starkey, DJ Shiftee, Liquid Stranger, EPROM, Bassbin Twins, Janover, TomFoolery. The promo copy called it "an independent festival for independent thinkers, dancers, creators and artists that brought EDM legends and emerging artists to thousands of revelers deep in the woods of the Midwestern United States," and described TomFoolery as founder of "CatalyticSouls, a crew responsible for hosting underground electronic and live band festivals in multiple states including the Cave Stock, CaveFest, and UnderGround Sound Series."

No Underground Sound 8 exists anywhere I could find: not on Resident Advisor, Last.fm, JamBase, The Untz, forums, or blogs. The Florida LLC was administratively dissolved on September 23, 2011, two months after UGS 7. Seven editions, 2005–2011, three venues.

### Gamma Fest, the website, and the roster

Two things the thesis leaves out that the archived website supplies. First, **Gamma Fest** (August 17–18, 2007): a two-night camp fest at Eureka Music Park in Eureka Springs, Arkansas, with Shpongle (DJ set) and Hallucinogen, co-branded with Simon Posford's Twisted Records, the crew's first out-of-state festival, five weeks after UGS 3 and while the thesis was being written. The same headliners came to the cave for UGS 4 a year later and Shpongle played UGS 7 as well.

Second, the **website**: catalyticsouls.com was live by February 4, 2005 as a phpBB portal (eight users, the catalyst/soul dictionary definitions on the front page, TomFoolery's monthly at Port Cape in Cape Girardeau and St. Louis gigs with the Nepol and ToyMaker crews). By mid-2006 the forum had 450 users and 1,028 posts; by November 2006 the member list passed 1,400, and the Wayback Machine holds roughly 2,100 archived threads. The 2006 About page called the site "your web connection to Caveman Bob and Gettin Lucky Productions," which means it was also the cave's public calendar. It listed the fall 2005 club tours (TechnoMeister; Face of Bass with Evol Intent; PsychoTronic with FACTORe in St. Louis, Memphis and Jackson; a New Year's Eve in St. Louis with Disco Productions) and gives UGS 1's exact dates as June 17–19, 2005, with George Acosta. A June 2007 thread was titled "Stabbing at the cave???" (content unrecovered). The June 2008 redesign added a Music Shop (UGS 4 will-call $40) and was never updated after UGS 4; the 2010–11 front page was a single UGS 7 teaser over a UGS 6 photo gallery, with a Joomla site at `/ugs/` ("Catalytic Souls Productions") still advertising the 2011 festival in January 2012. The domain was parked by 2013 and has served Japanese antiques spam since 2014.

The **Artists page** (2007, expanded June 2008) is the only roster the crew published: Tom Foolery ("one of the most recognized breaks DJs in the Mid-West... over twenty cities in twelve states... DEMF afterparties... followed Kaskade... peak hour at Tampa's Amphitheater"); Brian Dervish / Tekfro ("The Dervish"; Missouri punk bands from 1989, raves from 1994, mashups from 2002); DJ Andy B ("Wishes we would write his bio"); Unkl Ryan ("Is from outer space"; ten years of parties "all over the south, and Midwest"); DJ Derve ("Is not the Dervish"); Reperkushin (John; jungle and drum & bass since 2001, "a member of Catalytic Souls and Heavyweight Zoundz," turning to dubstep in 2008); Dave Skeezy; and, from 2008, DJ Brainstorm (drum & bass, Midwest Drum and Bass Alliance). Plus Spacehead (breaks, Carbondale, 2005) and DJ Fixx (label). Andy B, Tekfro, Derve, Reperkushin and Tommy I.C. Gunns were still on the UGS 7 bill in 2011. Real names for Andy B and Unkl Ryan were not found, and no successor crew in Carbondale or Murphysboro could be documented from the web.

### The 2007 press, found

The crew agent located the coverage the thesis complains about: The Southern Illinoisan, July 9 and 10, 2007 (Adam Testa), reported "at least four arrested during weekend festival" at UGS 3, and a private security firm, Five-"O," "contracted by cave owner Bob Goodale and the promoters of the Underground Sound Festival," said it had burned drugs and knives confiscated from about 15 people. The thesis's version ("several arrests were reported... however none were made at the venue"; three ambulance calls "mostly as a result of the extreme heat") and the paper's are not incompatible, but the burning-the-contraband detail explains why the sheriff's department was on television complaining about access.

## 3. Tom Hughes after Catalytic Souls (2011–2022)

- **Florida.** CATALYTICSOULS LLC was formed in Florida on December 29, 2008, with Thomas Hughes as manager and registered agent at a Fort Myers address (Orlando from the 2019 reinstatement). So he had relocated (or was relocating) within a year of the thesis, while still running UGS from a distance. His LinkedIn (read via a mirror) dates Fool'n'Around Sound August 2005–October 2010, CatalyticSouls LLC January 2006–August 2011, then Showmaster Production Logistics (2011–13), Bay Area Records (from July 2012), Full Sail faculty from January 2013 and Course Director from March 2015. His Mixcloud bio is a 2010–11 gig list: Grand Rapids, Minneapolis, the Arkansas Ozarks, St. Louis, Indiana, Miami, Florida. A 2011 St. Louis flyer bio reads "TomFoolery, based in Miami"; a later bio opens "From the beaches of Miami to the sands of the Playa" (Burning Man) and re-describes his sound as "bass music" rather than breaks. Both bios claim a Top 5 world ranking for breaks and #1 in "Acid Breaks" on The DJ List, a fan-vote site whose rankings are, to put it kindly, elastic.
- **Releases.** After the CatalyticSouls label went quiet in 2010 he released on Bay Area Records, 2012–2015: "Nothing Left" (with Lo IQ?), "Space Face Jake" (with Unter Liëben), "Sexy Bitch," "The Truth" (2012); "Let Your Mind Go" (2013); "Next Level," "Locked & Loaded" (with BBK), and *Excursions in Tech Vol. 1* under the TomFoolery/Zcarab pairing (2015). Discogs lists Zcarab and Hugues as his aliases. "Nothing Left" is on Spotify.
- **Full Sail.** His own blog (last updated March 2020) and LinkedIn describe him as "Course Director of Music Production at Full Sail University in Winter Park, Florida," with a bio that still leads with "EDM festivals, Underground Sound and Catalytic Souls, rave scene festivals held in a cave in Southern Illinois." The thesis's Foreword describes moving to Orlando in about 1999 hoping to attend Full Sail, failing to raise the money, and working food service at the Magic Kingdom instead. He ended up on the faculty.
- **dubTomFoolery.** A 2018 Bandcamp release, *listens back*, from Orlando: "hardware sequenced, generated and dub mixed in real time through miles of cable, tons of circuits, & a ghost 32 LE." The man who wrote a thesis about the laptop's conquest of the DJ booth ended his recording career on a hardware dub-techno rig. His Facebook page's last post, February 27, 2021: "Still love using tapes."
- **The LLC** was reinstated in December 2019, filed an annual report in January 2021, and was dissolved for non-filing in September 2022.
- **Death.** Reported by his collaborator on February 5, 2022, as above. He would have been 43.

## 4. The cave after Catalytic Souls (2008–2026)

The Shawnee Salt Petre Cave is at 3747 Illinois Route 127, about seven miles south of Murphysboro, on 50–70 acres abutting the Shawnee National Forest, with a 200-foot-plus sandstone bluff shelter that the Daily Egyptian in 2000 said had hosted "four or five concerts a year" from 1974 into the late 1980s (Ozark Mountain Daredevils, Steve Miller Band, Muddy Waters in 1978, Tanya Tucker at 15). Gib Todd of Murphysboro built it out; Bob Goodale, then an SIU student, helped organize the 1978 "Shawnee Jamboree" there. Goodale and his wife Lou bought it around 2000 ("We're bringing the cave back to the 70s"), reportedly cheaply after Goodale saved the prior owner's life. His site called it a non-profit with a capacity of 4,000. Nazareth played July 4, 2002.

**The Goodale decline (2009–2012).** After Catalytic Souls left, Goodale kept the 420 Show and the fall Harvest Fest going with regional jam bands and an electronic side stage through April 2011, plus a July 2011 "Caveblast." From 2008 his website carried a standing plea: "Help Save The Cave — Volunteers Needed / Forestry, Electrician Help Needed." A Sierra Club hiker in August 2010 found $5 admission, boating discontinued "due to insurance concerns," and an owner whose main business was firewood. The same hiker's postscript, August 31, 2012: gate closed, "for sale" sign. Shane Wade in 2023: "By probably 2008 or 2009 it shut down. My partner acquired it in 2012 or 2013 and then we just let it sit for a while, let the smoke clear from some of the stuff that happened there at the end. The city leaders had a bad taste in their mouths from that." The 2020 Southern Illinoisan reopening story called Goodale "the last face of the cave." (Bluegrass Today prints the purchase year as 2004; every other source, including the buyer's partner, contradicts that.) I found no obituary or current information for Goodale.

**The revival (2020–2024).** Jeff Parrish (owner) and Shane Wade (manager/co-owner, from Herrin, lives in Marion) spent the 2020 lockdown rebuilding: stage tripled to 50×50 feet, drainage, concrete for tour trailers, 20,000 square feet of turf, a liquor license, an RV park. Renamed the Shawnee Cave Amphitheater, it reopened October 31, 2020, with a socially distanced Yonder Mountain String Band show. Wade said he "didn't know the first thing about" the genre until someone told him to book Billy Strings; he became a jamgrass promoter by market research. The programming since: Weekend at the Cave (May 2021: Infamous Stringdusters, Sam Bush, Leftover Salmon, Keller Williams; June 2023: Greensky Bluegrass two nights, Twiddle, Pigeons Playing Ping Pong, SteelDrivers, Elephant Revival); Blues, BBQ & Soul (July 2021: Blind Boys of Alabama, Samantha Fish, Eric Gales, Dumpstaphunk, North Mississippi Allstars); a Total Solar Eclipse Festival (April 5–8, 2024, the cave being inside the path of totality); Shawnee Cave Revival (June 7–9, 2024: Greensky, Red Clay Strays, Grace Potter, Samantha Fish, Marc Broussard, Andy Frasco, Paul Thorn; billed as alcohol-free with ~3,500 camper capacity). Wade's stated ceiling: "my events are less than three thousand people... I like it at three." He described the audience as "kind of a hippy crowd, a lot of jam bands. There's a little bit of electronic but not too much."

That last line is the cave's verdict on its own 2000s. The venue that Hughes described as the last place in the Midwest where an all-night rave could legally run now programs "a little bit of electronic but not too much," to a crowd that, in Wade's telling, left a leased parking field with "not one piece of trash."

**Dark again (August 2024–present).** On August 6, 2024, the amphitheater cancelled Weekend at the Cave (August 30–September 1: Disco Biscuits, Railroad Earth, Galactic, Matisyahu, Steep Canyon Rangers), announced refunds, thanked everyone, gave no reason, and said it was closed for the season. As of September 2026, Songkick, JamBase, Vivid Seats and TicketSmarter show zero upcoming events; the last event any of them lists is that cancelled weekend. The venue's website fails its TLS handshake. A Facebook group thread asks "What happened to the Shawnee Cave Amphitheater?" No sale has been reported. It has now been closed for longer since the 2024 cancellation than it was open under Goodale's Catalytic Souls years.

| **deadmau5**, the Beatport curiosity | Seven Grammy nominations, the Cube, Kx5 with Kaskade, then a $55M sale of his catalog and mau5trap to Create Music Group in 2025. Retired the Cube in October 2025 and announced his first album in a decade for 2026. |
| **Infected Mushroom**, UGS 2 headliner | Still touring at Coachella and Tomorrowland scale; released *IM30* January 30, 2026. |
| **Electric Skychurch**, UGS 2 headliner | James Lumb continued sporadically; no significant 2020s activity found. |
| **Beatport**, "$1.99... not taking on any new labels" | Bought by SFX for ~$50M (2013), nearly sold at auction in SFX's 2016 bankruptcy, retained under LiveStyle, pivoted to streaming-into-DJ-software (Beatport LINK, 2019), bought Loopmasters and Plugin Boutique (2020) and a majority of IMS (2023). Tracks now $1.29–$2.49. |
| **Symphonic-style digital distribution** | Now the default route to market for every independent label; the "approval process" gatekeeping the thesis describes is gone. |
| **Phylis Johnson**, major professor | SIU Emeritus Professor of Sound and New Media; Professor at San José State; founding co-editor of *Resonance* (UC Press). |
| **SIU's own copy of the thesis** | Not findable in OpenSIUC (whose theses largely begin 2009–10) or by web search. Likely in ProQuest or the Morris Library catalog only. Your .docx may be one of very few copies in circulation. |

## 6. The thesis's claims and predictions, scored

| Thesis claim (2007) | What happened |
|---|---|
| Vinyl for dance music is dying; 300–600 sales per 12" is a collapse. | 2006 was the all-time bottom for US vinyl (858k LPs). It grew every year after: $1.04 billion and 46.8 million units in 2025, 19 consecutive years of growth, more than three times CD units. A 300-unit pressing is now the *standard, profitable* indie dance run because of pre-orders and Bandcamp, not a sign of failure. What died instead was the **download**: US download revenue fell to $221.8M in 2025, about a fifth of vinyl. Juno Download shut down in June 2026. |
| Digital stores (Beatport) are the future of dance distribution. | Right for about a decade, then overtaken by streaming. Beatport survived by becoming a subscription service piped into Rekordbox and Serato, plus a software company. The IMS Business Report put the global electronic music industry at $15.1B in 2025, up from ~$4B when Forbes started counting in 2012. |
| Start a zero-cost digital label to gather data, then press vinyl in Europe as a booking tool. | The label ran 14 releases in three years and never pressed vinyl. The playbook itself became the universal one via DistroKid, Bandcamp and Symphonic; the "European vinyl for prestige" leg is exactly what small dance labels do now, at 300 copies. |
| "The move to digital... has killed a huge part of the industry" (McBride) vs. "it allows new producers to effectively get out there" (Brea). | Both. Thousands of vinyl-era labels died. Symphonic alone now services half a million artists. The thesis's own third position, that the boom in bedroom producers would push production standards up "exponentially," was correct and is now the industry's core complaint (too much music, too little shelf life). |
| Track shelf life has collapsed to days because of file sharing. | Shelf life collapsed further, for a different reason: algorithmic streaming and TikTok. In 2023 the four most-popular songs on US TikTok were all sped-up remixes. |
| Producer/DJs have taken over; non-producing DJs are second class; ghost production is normal. | Fully realized. Ghost production is an open industry with published price lists ($300–2,500 a track). Stems separation in Serato, Rekordbox and Traktor (2022–24) means every DJ now does live remixing that in 2007 required Ableton and a weekend of tutorials. AI generators (Suno, Udio) settled with two majors in late 2025 and will do the ghost producer's job for pennies. |
| Laptops and CDJs replace vinyl in the booth; Richie Hawtin is the vanguard. | The laptop lost too. The booth standardized on Pioneer (now AlphaTheta) CDJs and USB sticks; Traktor, Hawtin's platform, is a niche product. New Zealand's competition regulator blocked AlphaTheta from buying Serato in 2024 specifically because it would have combined the dominant hardware and dominant software. Vinyl-only DJing survives as a deliberate aesthetic niche, which is exactly the "prestige" role the thesis predicted for vinyl records. |
| The rave has been forced into the club; the Midwest's warehouse scenes are dead; Shawnee Cave is the last legal all-nighter. | The 2010–2016 "EDM boom" was the rave going corporate rather than going club: Electric Daisy Carnival went from a Los Angeles rave to 525,000 people over three days in Las Vegas (2025) with Live Nation owning half of Insomniac; Robert Sillerman's SFX rolled up Disco Donnie, ID&T, Electric Zoo and Beatport, IPO'd in 2013, and was bankrupt by 2016. Then Gen Z reinvented the warehouse rave anyway (Boiler Room, hard techno at 150+ BPM, Berlin techno on the UNESCO heritage list in 2024), while nightclubs closed at record rates (UK down a third since 2020; Berlin's Watergate closed New Year's 2024). The Midwest camping rave the thesis defended became, in the 2010s, Electric Forest in Michigan, at 45,000 people. |
| Media sensationalism and the RAVE Act make promotion legally and financially precarious. | The RAVE Act has never produced a single prosecution, but its chilling effect was real enough that in 2018 the Department of Justice had to issue guidance saying free water and drug education don't violate it, after a campaign by Dede Goldsmith, whose 19-year-old daughter died at a 2013 DC show. DanceSafe now runs that campaign. 45 states legalized fentanyl test strips. Biden never amended his own law. Meanwhile the crack-house statute the thesis said was "thrown out" in New Orleans was used to seize Camp Zoe in 2010, the site of UGS 5 and 6. |
| Liquor-license holders and local politics kill unlicensed events. | Shane Wade's 2023 explanation for why the cave sat empty for a decade: "The city leaders had a bad taste in their mouths." The revived venue got a liquor license and programs bluegrass. |
| Crossover hits keep the underground economy afloat. | Inverted. From 2012 the crossover *was* the economy (Skrillex's Grammys, Calvin Harris's $66M years), and the underground defined itself against it. Hard techno and drum & bass had genuine US crossover moments in 2024. |
| Performance rights societies are nearly worthless for EDM. | Still largely true for DJ-tool tracks, though streaming royalties and sync are now real revenue lines, and Beatport's own data feeds label accounting in ways that didn't exist in 2007. |
| The DJ is a "conduit," the crowd is the performance, the event "exists only through the thoughts and actions of the participants." | This is the part that aged best, and it is the part that isn't about business. It is what Wade is selling at 3,000 capacity ("There's not a bad seat in the house"), it is what "intimacy" means in every post-COVID festival pitch, and it is why the thesis's least-quantifiable conclusion, "the right to be undefined," reads better in 2026 than most of its numbers. |

## 7. The wider record, condensed (2008–2026)

This section compresses the industry agent's ~80-source report (`agent-reports/03-edm-industry-2007-2026.md`) to the beats that matter for reading the thesis. Numbers and sources are in that file.

**Vinyl and retail.** Record Store Day launched in 2008 with ~1,400 US indie stores; there are ~2,000 now. Hard Wax lost its Berlin home in 2023 and moved into the Tresor building; Phonica opened a Berlin branch; Satellite Records became a download site and disappeared. The 2021–22 pressing crisis (300 million units on order against 150 million a year of capacity, after the Apollo Masters lacquer fire) hit indie dance labels with eight-month waits. Bandcamp changed hands twice (Epic Games 2022, Songtradr 2023, half the staff laid off) but Bandcamp Fridays paid artists $154M through 2025.

**The boom.** 2012 was the pivot: Skrillex's three Grammys, Forbes' first "Electronic Cash Kings" list, Sillerman relaunching SFX. EDC's general admission went from $180 (2011) to ~$500 (2025). Ultra generates ~$79M a year for Miami. The 2013 Electric Zoo deaths reintroduced the drug frame; the 2023 Electric Zoo collapse (unpaid vendors, oversold Sunday, four class actions, Avant Gardner bankrupt in 2025) closed the era.

**The bust and the reset.** Seven festivals cancelled for 2016; SFX bankrupt February 2016; "dance music has plateaued." COVID cut the industry 54% in 2020. Since: 100+ festival cancellations worldwide in 2025, one in five UK independent festivals gone since 2023, insurance at ~18% of budgets versus 6% pre-pandemic. Pollstar's top-100 average ticket was $132.62 in 2025. On April 15, 2026, a federal jury found Live Nation/Ticketmaster liable on every antitrust count in the states' case; remedies are pending. (Hughes worked as a stagehand for SFX and Clear Channel Entertainment, which became Live Nation in 2005.)

**Law.** The UK's "repetitive beats" clause is still law; £10,000 fines for unlicensed events arrived in 2020. NYC repealed its 1926 Cabaret Law in 2017. The Amend the RAVE Act campaign got DOJ clarification (2018) but no amendment.

**Technology.** CDJ-3000 (2020), AlphaTheta rebrand (2024), CDJ-3000X (2025). Spotify returned to DJ software in September 2025. Winter Music Conference, "a gathering of over 20,000" in the thesis, went on a five-year hiatus after 2019 and returned in 2025–26 as a much smaller event; Amsterdam Dance Event drew a record 600,000 in 2025.

**Scholarship.** Hughes wrote that he was "pleased that I have contributed to what appears a steady but slow growing pool of research." *Dancecult: Journal of Electronic Dance Music Culture* launched in 2009, two years later; Matos's *The Underground Is Massive* (2015) wrote the American history the thesis wished existed; Garcia-Mispireta's *Together, Somehow* (2023) and the *Oxford Handbook of Electronic Dance Music* (2021) made it a field.

## 8. Reading the thesis in 2026: what it got right, what it couldn't see

**Right:**
- That the DJ/producer merger was permanent and would restructure who gets booked.
- That digital distribution would democratize entry and flood the market, and that the response would be a rising production floor.
- That vinyl would survive as a prestige object rather than a distribution medium.
- That legal risk for promoters comes less from the law as written than from local politics, and that one bad news cycle can end a venue. (It took a few more years, but that is what happened to the cave.)
- That the camping-festival "multiple peaks and valleys, genre changes every hour or two" programming model was where audience attention was going. That is now the default format from Electric Forest to Shambhala.
- That branding (the name in lasers) mattered more than slot position. The thesis's account of taking the after-sunset Friday slot nobody wanted is a genuinely good piece of promoter craft.

**Wrong, or at least backwards:**
- Vinyl's economics. He was writing at the exact bottom.
- The download store as the destination. It was a way station.
- That raves were being pushed permanently into clubs. The opposite happened; clubs are what's dying.
- That "the ecstasy rage seems to have subsided." 2013 and fentanyl said otherwise, though his structural point (the media covers drugs, not music) held.
- The four-city club circuit and the 1,000 free CDs as a path to booking demand. That model was already being replaced by SoundCloud (launched the year after the thesis) and then by Instagram followings.

**Couldn't see:**
- That the crew itself would leave the cave within eighteen months of the thesis.
- That the two venues UGS moved to afterward were, respectively, about to be seized by the DEA and the home of the Gathering of the Juggalos.
- Bass music. The thesis is a breaks partisan's document, written when breaks were "almost taboo" in the Midwest; three years later his own festival was headlined by Excision and Bassnectar, and by 2011 his bio said "bass music."
- Smartphones, social media and the death of the flyer. The thesis's most vivid production passages are about paper flyers and a 40-mile distribution radius.
- That he would end up teaching at Full Sail.

**A note on the thesis as a source.** It anonymizes the Chicago promoter, the "true Midwest" rival festival, and the friend who leaked information; it reports Goodale's numbers as fact; and its attendance figures (1,200, 1,400, 2,000+) are the author's. The independently documented figures are consistently a bit lower (the Southern's ~800 projection for Cave Fest 2005; Strip Mines' 550 paid in 2008). None of that makes it unreliable; it makes it a memoir with footnotes, which is what its own Statement of Purpose says it is.

## 8b. Second-pass addenda (September 16–17, 2026)

A second research pass, run after the first draft, changed or sharpened the following:

- **The 2007 press, in full.** The Southern's July 9, 2007 story (Adam Testa, "Behavior in local cave drawing ire") is the outside account of UGS 3: a neighbor on Tomcat Hill Road growing his field knee-high to keep campers out; Sgt. Dave Nichols saying ambulances get called "every time they have that particular type of festival"; three ambulance runs, five hospital patients, at least four drug arrests; Goodale putting it on growth ("from about 600 people to more than 2,000... A few bad apples spoil it") and heat; deputies unable to patrol private land; a 17-year-old from Chicago chased into the woods and located by phone. The July 10 follow-up quoted Dennis Karnes of Five-"O" Event Security, hired by Goodale and the promoters, saying keeping contraband out "would have been impossible" and that the firm had burned what it confiscated. The thesis's account and the paper's agree on the facts and differ on the frame, which is the thesis's media argument in miniature.
- **Cavetronic, July 17–18, 2009.** The year after Catalytic Souls left, a Clarksville, Tennessee crew, Electronic Assault, with partners "SSI," ran an electronic camp-out at the cave with DJ Shortee and DJ Ronin ("representin Mid Tenn and N. Utah"); a promo clip calls it "Cavetronic 2," and a Nashville DJ's bio places the series in summer 2008 to winter 2009. So the rave era at the cave outlived the crew that built it by at least a summer, under out-of-state promoters, which is the likeliest referent for Shane Wade's "2008 or 2009... got out of hand."
- **The video record.** 57 YouTube clips survive: one walk-through of UGS 2; eighteen clips of UGS 3 (DJ Funk, Aphrodite, Jackal & Hyde, Reid Speed, DJ Karizma at dawn, a Doormouse live set, the fire troupe; one captioned "Hands down the best party I've ever been to"); one of UGS 4; twelve of UGS 5 (Bassnectar, Astral Projection, lasers, fireworks); twelve of UGS 6 (Rabbit in the Moon 3D, and Benji Ramsey's promo describing a Thursday disco party, "soundcamps," and a main stage "from dusk to dawn"); four of UGS 7 including Andy B's BNasty duo at 4 a.m. on the Freak Stage; six of Cavetronic; one of Cave Fest 2011.
- **Taper recordings.** The Live Music Archive holds eleven audience and soundboard recordings from the cave: David Nelson Band (April 2001), JEB at a "Shawnee Endless Summer Fest" (August 2002), Cornmeal on both nights of Cave Fest 2006, The New Ledge Band at Cavestock III (2006), Euforquestra and Family Groove Company at Stripminesfest (September 2007), Jaik Willis and the Super American Happy Fun Good Time Jamband at Cavestock 4 (October 2007), and Ultraviolet Hippopotamus at Strip Mines 2008. Nothing electronic; the DJ sets survive only on YouTube and in Brian Dervish's three Last.fm uploads (U Lounge 2006, Gamma Fest 2007, Cavestock 4).
- **People.** Andy B continued as half of BNasty (with Nasty Nate), playing Tres Hombres in Carbondale in July 2012, with a Facebook page in 2015; he is the crew member with the longest documented local presence, and an "Andy" administers the 1,250-member public Facebook group "UnderGround Sound Festival UGS7--- Fan Page," created August 16, 2010. Reperkushin is Jonathan Hamilton per Facebook, with 2006 Dogs On Acid radio billings as "the midwest bad boy killa... representing the Heavyweight Zoundz." Benji Ramsey is now a home inspector and "touring VJ." Spoololife, whose 2005 crew page carried a Tom Foolery bio, turns out to be a national collective, not a local one.
- **Hughes' later music.** As Zcarab he is co-credited with Green Velvet on "Move Your Body" on *Unshakable* (Relief Records, October 28, 2013), his most widely distributed record. His SoundCloud shows techno and acid sketches in 2016 and a run of dub and dancehall productions in 2017 ("Brukhall Rock," "Babylon Is Falling"), before the 2018 Bandcamp dub-techno piece whose notes call the project "the majestic dub ship Catalytic," the last use of the crew's name in his work.
- **The crew's own forum, recovered.** The Wayback scrape indexed 451 threads and saved about 150 before the archive went down. They give UGS 1's full bill (George Acosta, Craze, Doc Martin, Micro, Nigel Richards, Dylan, DJ Lee, FACTORe, Evol Intent, plus "Free Agent" and "Outlaw" satellite stages run by Kansas City and Wisconsin crews, so the crew-of-crews model predates the thesis's account by a year); the UGS 2 announcement in full ("the first to make advances are often cast out by society and referred to as freaks... Now we come together to receive the response"); Tom's answer to a 17-year-old ("This event is 17+. Be smart and be discrete"); an attendee's UGS 1 memory of "listening to Dylan spin DnB as the sun came up"; the April 2005 clean-up crisis ("Bob (the owner) and his wife were left to clean up a shitload of garbage"); CaveStock 2's flyers delayed by Hurricane Katrina hitting the designers in Jackson, Mississippi; and the crew's club life (TomFoolery's "USAmnesia" Thursdays at the Skyy Box, Spacehead's Ghetto Puff Productions, "Grave Dancing" with the Chicago Intergruv DJs). Reperkushin signed his posts "Jon Hamilton."
- **Goodale's own site, recovered.** cavefest.fws1.com's 324 captures date the "Help Save The Cave — Volunteers Needed" banner to March 2008 at the latest; list an October 13, 2007 "Freakenstein" night that Catalytic Souls hosted "to show our appreciation for all of the love and support given to us over the years"; show the crew and Gettin' Lucky co-presenting "Halloween at the Cave" on October 31, 2008, two months after UGS 4, which narrows the split to the winter of 2008–09; and end with a 2011 "420 Show" whose second stage was dubstep booked through Digital-Glitch. The host shows the site "disabled due to billing issue" by 2023.
- **Flyers.** The UGS 3 flyer (a casino-chip "777 · The Freaks Come Out" design listing Uberzone, Aphrodite, Jackal & Hyde, Skylab2000, DJ Funk, Reid Speed, Keith MacKenzie, FACTORe), the 2008 Cave Fest flyer with both stage lineups, the Halloween 2008 flyer, the UGS 5 "Freak'in Out" poster and the UGS 6 "Freakadelic" poster were all recovered and are on the wiki's media page with seventeen UGS 6 gallery thumbnails.
- **Still unreachable.** Reddit (two r/aves threads: a 2008 UGS 4 photo post and a "Cave Rave" memories thread whose snippet reads "There used to be Underground Sound at Shawnee cave in Murphysboro Illinois from about 2000 until about 2008. It was a huge..."), TranceAddict's UGS 4 thread, Dogs On Acid, Hip Forums, the Facebook group's posts, and the paywalled bodies of the Southern's 2005–2008 features. Three Wayback scrapes (forum threads, MySpace profiles, Goodale's site) were running as this was written; their reports are in `raw/`.
